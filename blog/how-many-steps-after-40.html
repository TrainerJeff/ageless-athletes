Do You Really Need 10,000 Steps a Day? What the Research Says After 40

10,000 steps is the number everyone knows. It didn't come from a lab. According to the University of North Carolina's public health school, it grew out of a decades-old marketing campaign for a Japanese pedometer. (UNC Gillings School of Global Public Health)

So what does the research actually say?

See your doctor first. This is general fitness education, not medical advice. If you have a heart, joint, or balance condition, check with your doctor before ramping up your activity.

The big picture: about 7,000 is a strong target

A 2025 review in The Lancet Public Health pooled 57 studies on device-measured steps. Compared with 2,000 steps a day, 7,000 steps a day was linked to:

  • 47% lower risk of death from any cause
  • 25% lower risk of cardiovascular disease
  • 38% lower risk of dementia
  • 22% lower risk of depressive symptoms
  • 14% lower risk of type 2 diabetes
  • 28% lower risk of falls (very low certainty evidence)

For death, heart disease, dementia, and falls, the benefit curve started to flatten around 5,000 to 7,000 steps. The authors said 10,000 is still fine if you're already active, but 7,000 "might be a more realistic and achievable target for some." (Ding et al., Lancet Public Health, 2025)

Age changes the number

An earlier analysis of 15 studies and nearly 50,000 people found the drop in risk of early death leveled off at about 6,000 to 8,000 steps a day for adults 60 and older, and about 8,000 to 10,000 for adults under 60. Walking speed didn't add a clear benefit beyond total steps. (UNC summary of Paluch et al., Lancet Public Health)

Even a few good days a week count

A study of 13,547 women (average age about 72) who wore step counters for a week and were followed for about 11 years found that hitting 4,000+ steps on just 1 to 2 days a week was linked to 26% lower risk of death compared with 0 days. Hitting it 3+ days a week was linked to 40% lower risk. More steps were still better. (British Journal of Sports Medicine, 2025)

The fine print

  • These are observational studies. They show links, not proof that steps alone caused the benefit.
  • The Lancet review authors flagged limits: few studies for some outcomes, no age-specific analysis, and possible confounding.
  • Steps are one piece. They don't replace strength training.

How to use this

  1. Find your baseline. Wear a step counter for a normal week. A phone works, or a simple fitness tracker or clip-on pedometer if you don't carry your phone.
  2. Add 1,000 a day. If you're at 3,000, aim for 4,000. That's about a 10-minute walk.
  3. Work toward 6,000 to 8,000 if you're 60+, or 7,000 to 10,000 if you're younger.
  4. Don't sweat a low day. Several decent days a week still counts.
  5. Wear shoes you'll actually walk in. Comfortable, supportive walking shoes make the habit easier.

Bottom line

  • 10,000 steps isn't magic. It started as marketing.
  • About 7,000 a day is linked to big health benefits. After 60, the benefit for longevity levels off around 6,000 to 8,000.
  • If you're low now, every extra 1,000 helps. Even 1 to 2 good days a week is linked to lower risk.

The Best Exercise for Your Bones After 40? A Big New Review Says Brisk Walking

After 40, bone density slowly starts to decline, and for many women the decline speeds up after menopause. Low bone density raises the risk of fractures, and a broken hip later in life can change everything.

Exercise helps. The harder question has always been which exercise, and how much. A large review in The BMJ gives one of the most detailed answers so far. NEJM Clinician covered it on October 7 with a one-line takeaway: "Brisk walking or jogging was the most effective option."

See your doctor first. This article is general fitness education, not medical advice. If you have osteoporosis, osteopenia, a past fracture, or take bone medication, talk to your doctor or a physical therapist before changing your exercise. Some movements, like heavy spinal flexion or high-impact jumping, may not be right for everyone.

The study

"Effect of exercise on bone health in middle aged and older adults: hierarchical network meta-analysis of randomised trials" was published in The BMJ on September 9, 2026. It pooled 124 randomized trials with 18,429 participants aged 40 and older. The researchers compared exercise types (walking or jogging, resistance training, combined aerobic and resistance, mind-body exercise such as tai chi or yoga, and others) and measured bone mineral density (BMD) at the lower spine, femoral neck, and total hip. Fractures were a secondary outcome.

What they found

  • Lower spine: Brisk walking or jogging and combined aerobic plus resistance exercise probably improved BMD.
  • Femoral neck: Brisk walking or jogging and mind-body exercise may give the greatest benefit.
  • Total hip: Only brisk walking or jogging stood out.

Brisk walking or jogging was the only option that showed up as helpful at all three sites. For fractures (26 trials, about 11,000 people), mixed aerobic exercise and mind-body exercise may lower risk, but that evidence was rated low certainty.

How much is enough?

The authors estimated that meaningful benefits might start at about 400 MET-minutes per week for the spine and about 600 for the hip. They describe 600 MET-minutes as roughly 2 to 3 hours a week of brisk walking or jogging. The dose-response curve was generally an inverted U: more helped up to a point, but more wasn't always better.

The caveats (and the pushback)

  1. Much of the evidence was rated low to moderate certainty. The authors call their findings "cautious support."
  2. The changes in density were small, which is typical. Bone changes slowly.
  3. Other researchers disagree. A published response on The BMJ site argues that the review pooled very different, mostly lower-quality studies and misclassified exercise intensity. Those authors point to high-quality trials in which high-intensity resistance and impact training built bone.

The fair reading: brisk walking or jogging is a solid, accessible base. Strength training still matters, both for bone and for the muscle and balance that help prevent falls.

A bone-friendly week for most healthy adults over 40

Brisk walking or jogging, about 2-3 hours a week. For example, 30-35 minutes, five days a week. "Brisk" means you're breathing harder but can still talk. Hills and stairs add load.

Strength training, 2 days a week. Squats or sit-to-stands, hip hinges, rows, presses, and loaded carries. Work up to weights that feel challenging for 6-12 reps with good form.

Balance or mind-body work, 2-3 short sessions a week. Tai chi, yoga, single-leg stands, heel-to-toe walking.

Sports count, too. Pickleball adds quick steps and direction changes, and golf adds lots of walking if you skip the cart.

Nutrition and testing: talk to your doctor

Calcium, vitamin D, protein, and sometimes medication also matter. Ask your doctor whether you need a bone density (DXA) scan and whether supplements make sense for you.

Bottom line

  • Across 124 trials, brisk walking or jogging was the most consistent exercise for bone density in adults over 40.
  • Meaningful benefits may start at about 2-3 hours a week.
  • Mind-body and mixed aerobic exercise may lower fracture risk (low certainty).
  • Keep lifting, and get guidance if you have low bone density.

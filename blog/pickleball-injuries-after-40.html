Pickleball After 40: The Injuries That Actually Happen (and How to Stay on Court)

Pickleball is great exercise and a lot of fun. It is also sending a lot of people over 40 to the doctor. The good news: the injury patterns are predictable, which means a lot of them are avoidable.

See your doctor first. This is general fitness education, not medical advice. If you have a heart condition, joint problem, osteoporosis, or balance issues, check with your doctor before taking up a new sport. If you get hurt, get it looked at.

How common are pickleball injuries?

A national study of U.S. emergency department data estimated pickleball injuries rose from about 1,313 in 2014 to about 24,461 in 2023. Most injuries were in people aged 60 to 79. (Sports Health, 2025)

A 2026 study from one large urban health system (545 patients, 2015 to 2024) found injuries rose every year, including a 386% jump from 2022 to 2023. The average patient was about 59, and 62% were aged 50 to 69. Most people first showed up at an orthopaedic clinic, not the ER. (JAAOS Global Research & Reviews, 2026)

The injuries that show up most

In that 545-patient study, the most common diagnoses were:

  • Arthritis flare-ups (16%)
  • Meniscus tears in the knee (11%)
  • Wrist fractures (5.7%)
  • Achilles tendon ruptures (5.5%)
  • Elbow tendon pain (epicondylitis, 5.1%)

Age matters. In the national ER data, players aged 60 to 79 had higher odds of wrist and hand injuries and fractures, and their injuries were about twice as likely to come from a fall. Players aged 40 to 59 had higher odds of lower-body injuries. Women were more likely to be hurt in falls and to have fractures; men had more strains and sprains. The study also recorded 5 deaths from cardiac arrest. (Sports Health, 2025)

The first month is the danger zone

A case series of 28 people who ruptured an Achilles tendon playing pickleball found an average age of about 64. Sixty-eight percent were hurt within their first month of playing, and 32% on their very first day. Only 47% returned to the sport, and almost all who didn't said they were afraid of getting hurt again. (Journal of the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, 2025)

That's a small study, but the message is clear: the risk is highest when your enthusiasm is ahead of your conditioning.

How to stay on court

1. Ramp up slowly

Yale Medicine sports doctors say to ease in and not play "too much, too fast, too soon." Start with fewer games, take breaks when you're tired, and add time as you get fitter. (Yale Medicine)

2. Warm up for 5 to 10 minutes

Use dynamic moves, not long static holds: lunges, big arm circles, knee lifts, an easy jog. Save the static stretching for after. (Yale Medicine)

3. Wear court shoes, not running shoes

Running shoes are built for moving forward. Pickleball is side to side. Yale's doctors recommend a tennis-type shoe that is secure and grips well. (Yale Medicine) Browse pickleball court shoes on Amazon.

4. Don't backpedal for lobs

Backpedaling on your heels for a lob is a common way older players fall. Turn and move to the ball instead. If you do go down, sports medicine doctors suggest rolling with the fall rather than throwing a hand out to catch yourself. That's how many wrist fractures happen. (Vitale & Liu, Current Sports Medicine Reports, 2020)

5. Check your paddle grip

A grip that's too big can strain your forearm. Get your hand measured or try a few sizes. (Yale Medicine) An overgrip can help you fine-tune the feel: browse pickleball overgrips on Amazon.

6. Train off the court

Yale's doctors recommend shoulder, arm, and core strength work plus balance practice, and cross-training like cycling or swimming. (Yale Medicine) A basic set of resistance bands covers rows, presses, and shoulder work at home. Slow calf raises on a step are an easy add for lower legs.

7. Know when to get checked

Sudden pain, a possible broken wrist or ankle, or pain that won't go away should be seen by a doctor or physical therapist. (Yale Medicine) Chest pain, unusual shortness of breath, or feeling faint means stop playing and get help now.

Bottom line

  • Most pickleball injuries in people over 40 are knees, wrists, Achilles, elbows, and arthritis flares.
  • Falls drive fractures, especially after 60. Don't backpedal.
  • The first month is when a lot of Achilles ruptures happen. Ramp up slowly.
  • Court shoes, a real warm-up, and some strength work off the court go a long way.
